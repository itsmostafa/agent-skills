# The Categories of Legitimate Reservation (CLR)

Eight tests for deciding whether a stated cause-and-effect relationship is real or just plausible-sounding. They are the difference between *somebody's perception* of a relationship and an *accurate representation of reality*. Use them three ways: to build your own reasoning soundly, to self-check a conclusion before you act on it, and to challenge a claim (yours, the user's, a source's) **without being adversarial** — a "reservation" is a request for more information, not an accusation. Verbalize it as *"I have a reservation about the link between X and Y…"* and name the category.

Two framing rules the whole method rests on:
- **Correlation is not causation.** Two things moving together is an observation, not a mechanism.
- **Necessary is not sufficient.** A cause can be genuinely required and still not be enough on its own.

Below, each test gives: the question to ask, how to apply it, and a worked example. The first three check the *statements and the basic link*; #4–#8 check *the nature of the causality* and are where most real errors hide.

---

## 1. Clarity — "Do I actually understand this?"

Always first. Clarity is about communication, not logic: settle what a claim *means* before judging whether it's *true*. **Seek to understand before seeking to be understood.**

Raise a clarity reservation when:
- The meaning, or the significance, of the statement isn't clear.
- The connection between the stated cause and effect isn't convincing at face value.
- **Intermediate steps are missing** — a "long arrow." The jump from cause to effect skips links that the speaker sees intuitively but hasn't stated. Long arrows are where hidden faulty assumptions live, so make the missing steps explicit.

*Example.* "My gasoline mileage deteriorates → a tune-up on my engine is complete" is a long arrow. Spelled out: my car is old → old cars are often tuned to minimize emissions → emission-minimizing tunes burn more fuel → mileage drops. Only once the steps are visible can you test them.

## 2. Entity Existence — "Is each statement a single, complete, real idea?"

Check each statement (each "entity") on its own, three ways:
- **Complete.** A grammatical sentence with a subject and verb. No bare fragments, no impersonal "it/this/those." "Economic recession" fails; "The country is in an economic recession" passes. It should read sensibly with "if" or "then" in front of it.
- **Structurally sound — one idea, no embedded if-then.** No compound statements ("The sky is falling *and* it hits Chicken Little"). Watch the *disguised* if-then hiding inside "**in order to…**" and "**…because…**": "We park in the garage in order to avoid weather damage" is really "*If* we park in the garage, *then* we avoid damage" — split it.
- **Valid.** The statement exists in reality and can be evidenced. "Most grass is green" passes; "The sky is falling" doesn't. (This validity test applies to *conditions*, not yet-to-happen *actions*.)

*Why it matters:* you cannot cleanly test a causal link when two ideas are fused into one box or when a cause is smuggled inside an effect.

## 3. Causality Existence — "Does this cause really produce this effect?"

Now test the *arrow*. Read it aloud, literally: **"If [cause], then [effect]."** If it doesn't make sense spoken exactly that way, the causality is suspect.

- Read only what's actually stated — not what you read into it. (Clarity first prevents most of this.)
- **Beware intangible causes.** If the cause can't be directly observed or measured ("my boss is dissatisfied with me", "customers don't like our product"), you can't confirm it directly. To trust it, you must find at least one *other* observable effect of that same cause — which is exactly test #7.

*Example.* "If the weather is hot and humid, then we can expect an earthquake" — read aloud, the link plainly doesn't exist. Contrast "If two cars collide at 60 mph, then both are destroyed."

## 4. Cause Insufficiency — "Is this cause enough by itself?" (missing AND)

The most common error in real reasoning. The stated cause is genuinely *part* of the story but can't produce the effect alone — an additional, unstated factor must be present at the same time. These co-required causes form a logical **AND** (all needed; remove any one and the effect disappears).

- Ask: *"Can this cause produce the effect on its own, or must something else be true at the same time?"*
- Watch for **"oxygen"** — a cause so basic to the situation that it's transparent to you but not stated: "If we have fuel and a heat source, then fire" forgets *oxygen*. Sometimes oxygen is a safe constant to omit; sometimes it's a real variable you've silently assumed. Decide consciously.
- Rule of thumb: if a single arrow is carrying a big effect all by itself, be suspicious. If you need more than ~3–4 co-causes, some of them are probably *independent* causes — go to #5.

*Example.* "If we have a heat source, then we have steam power" is insufficient. It needs, in concert: a heat source **and** water **and** a closed vessel.

## 5. Additional Cause — "Could something else produce this effect on its own?" (missing OR)

A separate, **independent** cause could produce the same effect all by itself. This is an **OR**, not an AND. The scrutinizer isn't disputing your cause — only pointing out another one that would do it alone.

- Test: *"If I eliminate the stated cause entirely, is there any other circumstance under which the same effect — to the same degree — would still occur?"* If yes, there's an additional cause.
- **Magnitude matters.** An additional cause only counts if it produces the effect at roughly the magnitude in question. If everyone's sales fell 10% in a downturn but yours fell 20%, the downturn is a real additional cause for *part* of it — there's still something else explaining the other 10%.

*Consequence for action:* when an effect has independent OR-causes, removing just one **won't fix it** — the others still produce it alone. You have to address all the live ones. This is the single most common reason a fix "should have worked" but didn't.

*Example.* "Trash litters the yard" could be caused by dogs tipping the can **or** by wind blowing it over — independently. Stopping the dogs doesn't stop the wind.

## 6. Cause-Effect Reversal — "Is the arrow backwards?" (why vs. how-I-know)

A subtle, high-yield test. Distinguish *why* an effect happens from *how you know* it happens. The stated "cause" may actually be an **indicator** of the effect, not its source — the arrow points the wrong way.

- First signal is usually a gut feeling that the arrow's direction is off.
- Ask: *"Could the stated cause really be an indicator or symptom of the effect, rather than its source?"*

*Examples.*
- "If many fishermen line the bank and their stringers are full, then fishing is good." Backwards — full stringers are *how we know* fishing is good, not *why* it's good. The real cause is upstream: the river was stocked and season just opened.
- "If test scores are below the 50th percentile, then new students are poorly qualified." Low scores are an *indicator* of poor qualifications, not their cause.
- "If I have a fever and abdominal pain, then I have appendicitis." Backwards — the appendicitis causes the fever and pain, not vice-versa.

## 7. Predicted Effect Existence — "If this cause is real, what else must be true?"

The strongest tool for confirming or killing a claim, especially about an **intangible cause**. If the proposed cause genuinely exists, it will produce *other* effects besides the one in front of you. Go look for them.

- **To confirm:** name another effect the cause must also produce, then check that it's present. *"If appendicitis is really causing the pain, we'd also expect an elevated white-cell count and a fever — and we see both, so the cause holds."*
- **To refute:** name the other expected effect and show it's **absent**. *"If the engine were really flooded, we'd smell gasoline — we don't, so 'flooded' is refuted."*

Three ways a predicted effect settles the question: **Is it there?** (present when it shouldn't be, or absent when it should be, → refutes). **Can it coexist?** (if the two effects can't be true at once, the cause is suspect — the same cause under the same conditions can't produce both a profit and a loss). **Is it all there?** (present but at the wrong magnitude → the cause is insufficient or invalid).

Verbal form: *"If we accept that [cause] produces [effect], then it must also produce [predicted effect], which does / does not exist."*

## 8. Tautology — "Am I using the effect to prove the cause?" (circular logic)

The effect is offered as the evidence for the cause that supposedly produced it. Most tempting when the cause is intangible and no independent evidence is offered.

- Test: *Is the cause intangible? Is the effect being used as the rationale for the cause? Is there any independent, verifiable effect that could substantiate the cause instead?* If the only support for the cause is the very effect it's meant to explain, it's a tautology.

*Examples.*
- "The Dodgers lost because they played poorly." — "How do you know they played poorly?" — "They lost, didn't they?" (Ignoring that the pitcher may have carried a no-hitter into the 10th.)
- "The garlic and cross keep vampires away." — "How do you know they work?" — "You don't see any vampires, do you?"

The escape from tautology is the same move as #7: demand an *independent* predicted effect (errors, walks, hits — for the ballgame) rather than letting the outcome vouch for its own explanation.

---

## Fast self-scrutiny checklist

Run down this list against any load-bearing "X causes Y" before you act on it:

1. **Clarity** — Do I understand it? Any missing steps (long arrow)?
2. **Entity Existence** — One complete, real idea per statement? No hidden if-then inside "in order to / because"?
3. **Causality Existence** — Does "if X then Y" hold when read aloud? Is the cause intangible?
4. **Cause Insufficiency** — Enough alone, or is there an unstated AND-factor ("oxygen")?
5. **Additional Cause** — Would an independent OR-cause produce Y anyway? (Then fixing X won't be enough.)
6. **Cause-Effect Reversal** — Is X really just how I *know* Y, not why Y happens?
7. **Predicted Effect Existence** — What else must be true if X is real? Is it there, coexisting, at the right magnitude?
8. **Tautology** — Am I proving the cause with its own effect?

The categories that most often catch a wrong conclusion that "felt right": **4 (insufficiency)**, **5 (additional cause)**, and **6 (reversal)**. Spend your skepticism there.
